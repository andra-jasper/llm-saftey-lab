# Paper notes: Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models (GCG)

## Paper
Andy Zou, Zifan Wang, Nicholas Carlini, Milad Nasr, J. Zico Kolter, Matt Fredrikson.
"Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models."
arXiv:2307.15043 (v1: 27 Jul 2023; v2: 20 Dec 2023).
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043 (subjects: cs.CL, cs.AI, cs.CR, cs.LG).
Read 2026-10-19.

## One-paragraph summary
The authors ask whether adversarial suffixes against RLHF-aligned LLMs can be *automatically optimized* rather than hand-crafted, and whether a single suffix can be *universal* (works across many harmful behaviors) and *transferable* (works across models, including black-box commercial ones). Their method, Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), treats the suffix as a sequence of discrete tokens and optimizes it with a greedy coordinate-descent loop guided by gradients: for each suffix position, compute the gradient of the loss w.r.t. the token's one-hot indicator, keep the top-k candidate replacements, randomly sample a batch of candidate substitutions, evaluate each exactly, and apply the best. The loss targets an affirmative response start (e.g. "Sure, here's…"), exploiting the observation that refusal is a shallow behavior — once the model begins complying, it tends to continue. Optimizing jointly over many harmful prompts and multiple open models (Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B) yields suffixes that transfer to black-box public interfaces: ChatGPT, Bard, and Claude, plus open models like LLaMA-2-Chat, Pythia, and Falcon. Per-model attack success rates are in the paper's Table 1; the headline is that suffixes optimized only on open white-box models jailbreak commercial systems at non-trivial rates. This shifted the field's threat model from "jailbreaks need human creativity" to "jailbreaks can be automated, universal, and transferable," and made GCG the standard baseline attack that later defenses (SmoothLLM, RPO, perplexity filters) are benchmarked against.

## Key takeaways (for our work)
- **Direct motivation for A6 (perplexity filter).** GCG suffixes are gibberish token strings — optimizing for function, not naturalness. High-perplexity, low-naturalness text is exactly what a perplexity filter catches, which is why the A6 task exists next. This paper is the "why" behind it.
- **Honest coverage gap in our suite.** A2's 25 hand-written injection cases and A4's regex/keyword detector target *manual* attacks with meaningful payload text. GCG-style gibberish suffixes are a different attack class: A4's keyword features would likely miss them (no policy keywords in the suffix itself), and A2 contains nothing like them. When the A6 perplexity filter lands, re-evaluate: the right metric is "does perplexity catch what keywords miss?"
- **D5 design note (red-team/blue-team loop).** We can't run GCG here — no Vicuna weights, no gradients, no RLHF model, CPU only. Our planned gradient-free stand-in (random-search suffix generator vs. perplexity filter) is a deliberate, weaker proxy. Record honestly in D5 that random search ≠ GCG and that nothing learned about gradient-guided transfer applies to it.
- **Taxonomy placement (C7).** GCG defines the "automated / gradient-based" branch of the attack taxonomy: white-box (GCG) vs. black-box-transfer vs. manual jailbreaks (A3 corpus). Future follow-ups to file here: SmoothLLM (randomized perturbation defense), RPO (robust prompt optimization), and stealth-vs-transfer tradeoff papers.
- **Connection to B3 (Wei et al.).** GCG's affirmative-start trick and Wei et al.'s "competing objectives" framing are the same mechanism: safety training installs refusal as a shallow pattern, and attacks that break the first few tokens win.

## Limitations / open questions
- **White-box only at train time.** GCG needs gradients and many forward/backward passes on open models; it can't optimize directly against black-box APIs. Transfer works empirically but ASR varies by target — the paper doesn't give a theory of *why* these suffixes transfer, only that they do.
- **Detectability trade-off.** The suffixes look like noise; defenses that penalize unnaturalness (perplexity filters, paraphrase/retokenization) blunt them. Later work explores stealthier suffixes at the cost of ASR — an open design space we could probe offline (does *our* perplexity filter catch *our* random-search suffixes? D5).
- **Not replicated here.** We record no ASR numbers from this paper's tables in our own results — reproducing GCG requires model weights and gradients we don't have. This note is a study note, not a replication. A true replication would be: run the open-source GCG implementation on a small open model, log ASR on AdvBench — out of scope for CPU-only day-scale work, but a legitimate D-series candidate if GPU access ever arrives.
- **Open question for the flagship:** do gradient-based suffixes transfer *across defense layers* the way they transfer across models? I.e., does a suffix optimized against a perplexity filter beat a paraphrasing defense? That experiment is cheap offline and would be an original contribution.
