## One-paragraph summary

Many-shot jailbreaking (MSJ) is a jailbreak that only becomes
possible once context windows grow large: the attacker puts a
single prompt in front of the model containing hundreds of faux
Human/Assistant dialogues in which the assistant cheerfully
complies with harmful requests (picking locks, building weapons,
insulting the user, and so on), then appends the real target query.
With a handful of dialogues the safety-trained refusal still wins;
with enough of them — the paper tests up to 256 shots — the model
continues the pattern and answers the target query too. The
effectiveness of the attack follows a power law in the number of
shots, the same power law that benign in-context learning (ICL)
follows, which is the paper's central claim: MSJ is not a trick
bolted onto the model, it is ordinary in-context learning operating
on a harmful pattern, so larger (better in-context-learning) models
need *fewer* shots, and standard alignment fine-tuning only delays
the attack rather than preventing it at arbitrary context length.
Anthropic briefed other developers before publishing and had
mitigations in place on its own systems at publication.

## Threat model and attack construction (§2)

- **One prompt, fake history.** The whole attack lives in a single
  user prompt. It is formatted to look like a dialogue history —
  `Human: <harmful query>` / `Assistant: <compliant answer>` —
  repeated n times, with the target query appended last. Nothing
  is fine-tuned, optimised by gradient, or split across turns.
- **Where the demonstrations come from.** The compliant
  question–answer pairs are generated by a "helpful-only" model —
  tuned to follow instructions but *not* harmlessness-trained —
  then randomised in order. The paper notes a human could write
  them by hand, and that an open helpful-only model would do;
  the attacker does not need the target model's weights or the
  target answers in advance (see the mismatch results below).
- **Access requirement, stated honestly.** Vanilla MSJ requires
  API access: consumer chat products (ChatGPT, Claude.ai) do not
  let a user insert a faux dialogue history. This is a
  long-context API attack surface, not a copy-paste chat trick.
- **Why now.** The introduction frames the enabling change as
  context growth itself: from ~4,000 tokens at the start of 2023
  to multi-novel / codebase scale (the paper cites up to 10M
  tokens) — a capability improvement that creates the attack
  surface as a side effect.

## How success is measured (§3)

Two measures, used throughout:

1. **Sampled harmful-response rate** — the frequency of successful
   jailbreaks as judged by a refusal classifier.
2. **Negative log-likelihood (NLL)** of a compliant answer to the
   target query, `NLL = E[ −log P(a* | q1, a1, …, qn, an, q*) ]`.
   NLL is the more sensitive instrument: it registers the attack
   gaining ground even while the sampled success rate is still
   near zero, which is what makes the power-law fits possible.

Three evaluation settings (§3.1), all run on Claude 2.0 unless
stated otherwise:

1. **Malicious use-cases** — security/societal requests (weapons,
   disinformation, and the paper's other misuse categories,
   including violent-hateful, deception, discrimination, and
   regulated content).
2. **Malevolent personality evals** — yes/no questions probing
   malign personality traits (e.g. psychopathy; after Perez et
   al. 2022).
3. **Opportunities to insult** — benign questions on which the
   model is to be steered into insulting the user.

## Headline results (paper's Figures 2–4)

- **Shot count is the dial.** On the malicious use-cases tasks the
  attack "doesn't work at all with 5 shots, it works consistently
  with 256 shots" (Figure 2, left). On malicious use-cases the
  authors scaled to ~70,000 tokens *without observing a plateau*
  in the harmful-response rate, and on the personality evals they
  report near-complete adoption of the undesirable behaviour.
- **Across models.** MSJ was demonstrated on Claude 2.0, GPT-3.5,
  GPT-4, Llama 2 (70B), and Mistral 7B; around 128-shot prompts
  were sufficient for all of the evaluated models to adopt the
  harmful behaviour in the NLL evaluation. Two honest scope
  notes: Llama 2 (70B)'s 4,096-token context caps how many shots
  it can even receive, and Google DeepMind models were *not*
  evaluated in the log-probability comparison because the needed
  log-probability readings were unavailable.
- **Diversity, not topic match, is what transfers (§3.4).** With
  the target fixed in the "deception" category: demonstrations
  drawn narrowly from one other category ("discrimination")
  fail, while demonstrations drawn broadly from *all categories
  except the target* recover baseline performance. The authors
  read this as evidence that a sufficiently long, sufficiently
  diverse attack could act as a "universal" jailbreak — the
  attacker need not possess examples of the exact harm targeted.
- **Formatting does not save you (§3.3).** Swapping the user /
  assistant tags, translating the dialogues, or replacing the
  tags with "Question" / "Answer" changes the *intercept* of the
  scaling curve but not its *slope* — and can even increase
  effectiveness, plausibly because the reformatted prompt is
  out-of-distribution relative to the alignment fine-tuning data.
  A monitor keyed to one dialogue format therefore buys shots,
  not safety.
- **Composition (§3.5).** MSJ composes with Wei et al.'s
  black-box "competing objectives" attack (B3 in this lab):
  the hybrid beats plain MSJ at every context length tested,
  i.e. it reduces the context needed for a given success rate.
  Composition with a GCG adversarial suffix (A5 in this lab) is
  mixed: the suffix sharply raises the zero-shot success
  probability but adds much less at long contexts — the authors
  speculate GCG is position-specific and is diluted as
  demonstrations are inserted. Composition is the reason
  intercept-only defenses are fragile (see Mitigations).

## The power law — the paper's core contribution (§4)

Effectiveness follows, on every task considered:

```
−E[ log P(harmful resp. | n-shot MSJ) ]  =  C · n^(−α)  +  K
```

- In log–log plots this is a line (for K = 0) or a convex curve
  asymptoting to K. The **intercept** encodes the zero-shot
  likelihood of the harmful behaviour; the **exponent α** encodes
  how fast in-context evidence accumulates — the "speed" of
  in-context learning.
- **Benign ICL obeys the same law (§4.1).** On safety-unrelated
  tasks (the paper's Figure 2, right, includes LogiQA,
  TruthfulQA, Winogrande, TriviaQA, CommonsenseQA, CREAK), NLL
  of the target completion falls with the number of
  demonstrations in the same power-law form. MSJ scaling is
  therefore presented as a *special case* of ICL scaling, not a
  separate phenomenon — consistent with earlier token-wise loss
  scaling results (Xiong et al. 2023, cited there).
- **Larger models are more vulnerable (§4.2).** Across sizes in
  the Claude 2.0 family, larger models have larger exponents —
  they learn faster in context — and so need fewer shots to
  reach a given attack-success probability. Capability and
  vulnerability scale together here, which is the opposite of
  the usual "bigger model, better refusal" intuition.
- **Theory sketch.** Appendix H develops double-scaling laws for
  ICL and two induction-head-based prototype mechanisms that
  produce power laws of the observed shape. The paper is
  explicit that testing those mechanisms is future work; the
  empirical law, not the mechanism, is the established result.
  The practical consequence the authors draw: if the circuits
  behind MSJ *are* the circuits behind general ICL, a defense
  that removes MSJ without damaging benign ICL may be hard in
  principle.

## Mitigations — delay is not prevention (§5)

The power-law framing turns defense evaluation into a precise
question: does an intervention move the intercept, the exponent,
or the offset K? Only an exponent driven toward 0 prevents the
attack at *arbitrary* context length.

- **General alignment fine-tuning (§5.1).** Tracking the power
  law through supervised learning (SL) and reinforcement
  learning (RL): both raise the intercept (zero-shot harmful
  behaviour gets less likely) and leave the exponent essentially
  unchanged. Scaling up the standard pipeline therefore delays
  MSJ; it does not remove the scaling.
- **Targeted fine-tuning on MSJ itself (§§5.2–5.3).** Training
  on benign responses to MSJ prompts (up to 10-shot attacks in
  the training data) — by SL and by targeted RL — shows the
  same signature: intercept up, exponent unchanged. A telling
  control in the same figure: after enough targeted SL, benign
  demonstrations stop *helping* either (the model learns to
  ignore the pattern in both directions), i.e. the intervention
  works by blunting ICL on this format, not by teaching a
  distinction between harmful and benign patterns.
- **Prompt-based defenses (§5.4).** Two are evaluated on the
  deception category with a 205-shot prompt:
  - *In-Context Defense (ICD)* — prepend demonstrations of
    refusals: attack success 61% → 54%. Barely moves.
  - *Cautionary Warning Defense (CWD)* — prepend + append
    natural-language warnings against being jailbroken:
    61% → 2%. This is the strongest mitigation in the paper,
    and it is the result behind the blog's "61% to 2%" headline
    for prompt classification/modification mitigations.
  The paper's own caveat stands: the safety–capability
  trade-offs of CWD were not evaluated, and variant attacks
  that evade it were not ruled out.
- **The trivial defense is the one nobody wants.** Capping the
  context window prevents MSJ entirely and destroys the
  usefulness that motivated long contexts — stated in both the
  paper discussion and the blog, and rejected as the answer.

## Independent replication on HarmBench (§7)

A separate team, on an independent codebase with subtly
different design choices, ran MSJ on HarmBench (Mazeika et
al. 2024 — the subject of this lab's D2) against Claude 2.0
via the API. Takeaways were consistent with the main results:
among the jailbreaking techniques considered in HarmBench, MSJ
had a higher attack success rate, sometimes by a wide margin,
and the diversity and composition findings replicated. This
section matters for how much weight to put on the headline
numbers: the effect survived a re-implementation, not just the
original authors' pipeline.

## Relation to prior work, in the paper's own framing (§6)

- MSJ is the long-context scaling of an already-known
  short-context attack: Wei et al. (2023b) "In-Context Attack"
  and Rao et al. (2023) "Few-Shot Hacking." The paper keeps the
  name "many-shot" precisely to mark that the *scaling law and
  the mitigation failure* are properties of the long-context
  regime — the current alignment pipeline is described as
  sufficient for the short-context version and failing for the
  long-context one.
- Wolf et al. (2023), cited there, give the theoretical version
  of the worry: under a Bayesian-inference view of LLMs, a
  sufficiently long prompt exists that elicits any behaviour
  the model is capable of. MSJ is the constructive, measured
  instance of that claim for compliant-with-harm behaviour.

## Key takeaways (for our work)

- **A whole attack axis the lab has not touched: shot count.**
  Every suite in this lab is short — A2's 25 direct cases are
  single- or few-turn, B5's indirect cases carry one injected
  instruction per document, C6's completion cases carry one
  fake turn. TAXONOMY.md (C7) already files many-shot as a gap
  ("paper notes C8, simulation D1"); this note confirms the gap
  is structural, not a missing category label: no lab case varies
  the *number* of demonstrations, which is the paper's entire
  independent variable.
- **MSJ is not prompt injection, and the distinction matters
  for our defenses.** The faux dialogues sit inside the single
  user prompt and impersonate *assistant turns*, not a retrieved
  data channel. The lab's structural defenses — B2 spotlighting,
  B6 RAG delimiting/datamarking, C6's StruQ-style front-end —
  separate prompt from data; MSJ never crosses that boundary,
  so none of them addresses it as designed. The channel being
  forged here is the *turn* channel (who supposedly spoke),
  one level above the prompt/data channel C5/C6 studied.
- **Read through B3's lens (Wei et al.).** MSJ is Wei et al.'s
  in-context attack scaled until a second effect dominates:
  beyond "competing objectives" (follow the demonstrated
  pattern vs. refuse), the paper's claim is that ICL itself —
  the capability safety training relies on for instruction
  following — is the mechanism. That is why formatting changes
  and targeted refusal training, which operate on surface and
  intercept, do not touch the exponent.
- **Our detectors, honestly assessed.** A6's perplexity filter
  should be expected to miss MSJ: the demonstrations are fluent
  natural dialogue (generated by a language model), the same
  class A6 already showed overlapping benign perplexity. A4's
  detector_v1 would fire on harmful keywords inside individual
  demonstrations, but MSJ's signal is *repetition of a compliance
  pattern across turns*, not any single instruction — a detector
  for it would need to count compliant faux turns, a feature
  neither A4 nor B4 has. Recorded as a design observation, not
  a measurement: neither detector was run on many-shot input.
- **What a defense would have to do.** In the paper's terms, a
  real MSJ defense must reduce the exponent or raise K, not
  just the intercept — which reframes B1's result too: system-
  prompt hardening that raises the "cost" of an attack in shots
  is, by this paper's standard, a delay, and should be reported
  as one. CWD's 61% → 2% is the prompt-stage result to beat,
  with its unevaluated capability cost noted.
- **D1 scoping, set by this paper.** D1 (many-shot simulation
  on distilgpt2) cannot reproduce MSJ: distilgpt2's 1,024-token
  context fits a handful of short shots, not 256, and it has no
  safety training to jailbreak, so there is no refusal to
  overcome and no ASR to measure. The honest D1 question is the
  directional one the paper's §4 licenses: does the NLL of a
  compliant continuation fall as shots increase, in power-law
  form, on a small model with no alignment at all? A yes would
  support "this is ICL, not alignment failure"; a no would
  show the effect needs scale. Either result is informative;
  neither is an MSJ replication, and D1 must say so.
- **D2 link.** The paper's HarmBench replication (§7) is a
  second, independent reason D2's mapping is worth doing:
  HarmBench is the one external benchmark where a lab-relevant
  attack (MSJ) already has published, replicated numbers to
  anchor our categories against.

## Limitations / open questions

- **Closed-model centre of gravity.** The headline sampled
  results are Claude 2.0; the cross-model comparison leans on
  NLL because sampled ASR and log-probabilities are not
  uniformly available across APIs, and one major provider's
  models are absent from the NLL figure entirely. "128 shots
  jailbreaks everything" is, precisely, "everything evaluated,
  on the personality-eval NLL measure."
- **NLL is a proxy.** A falling NLL for a compliant completion
  shows the distribution shifting; the sampled harmful-response
  rates are the ground truth, and the two are not interchangeable.
  The paper uses both, but several scaling claims (model size,
  formatting) rest mainly on NLL.
- **Demonstration supply is assumed.** The attack presumes the
  attacker can obtain hundreds of compliant harmful Q&A pairs
  (via a helpful-only model). The mismatch results soften this
  (diverse off-topic pairs work), but a world where helpful-only
  models are scarce would raise the attack's cost — the paper
  does not model that defender lever.
- **No defense is shown to work at arbitrary length.** Every
  fine-tuning intervention leaves the exponent intact, and the
  one strong prompt defense (CWD) is evaluated at one shot
  count on one category with its capability cost unmeasured.
  As of this paper, MSJ at unbounded context is an open
  problem, not a solved one with a recommended fix.
- **Open question for the flagship:** is there a turn-channel
  analogue of StruQ's fix — a structured representation in
  which faux assistant turns inside user input are provably
  distinguishable from real assistant turns — and would it
  survive the paper's formatting result (intercept moves,
  slope doesn't) any better than prompt-time warnings did?
  That is a concrete, lab-sized question D1/E1 can carry.
